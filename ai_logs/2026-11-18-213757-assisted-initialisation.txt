  Third question: what goes into a pupil's percentage at a given date?

  This choice determines how the "as at" requirement is met.

  1. Percentage correct across the academic year so far (my recommendation).
     Count every eligible response from 1 September up to the as-at date. When
     the year group changes, the score starts afresh, which matters because
     we're reading each test against its year group's expectations. It also uses
     enough questions to be stable. Recomputing at any date just means
     filtering on answered_at.
  2. Percentage correct across all history up to the as-at date. This is the
     simplest option. However, it mixes a Year 3 percentage with a Year 4
     percentage, and older results hold the score down.
  3. The latest completed sitting only. This is closest to a real SATs, since
     SATs is one test. But a single sitting may cover only a few topics, so the
     score swings around.

  the README:

  - Survey sittings are excluded.
  - is_no_attempt counts as wrong. On a real SATs, a blank answer scores zero.
  - Adaptive sittings are excluded, because percentage correct isn't comparable

● Noted: the percentage covers the academic year to date, resetting each 1

  Fourth question: how should a percentage map onto 80–120?

  1. Seed table built from the GOV.UK conversion tables (my recommendation).
     Load the official 2025 KS2 raw-to-scaled tables for Maths and Reading as a
     dbt seed. Raw marks become percentages of the maximum mark, and the score
     is interpolated between those points. This keeps the real non-linear curve:
     the expected standard (100) sits at about 55–60%, and the curve flattens
     at both ends. It's easy to defend, and changing to a new year's table only
     means updating the seed.
  2. A straight line, where 0% gives 80 and 100% gives 120. This is trivial to
     build but wrong: 50% would land on 100, below where the real
     expected-standard threshold sits.
  3. Three linear segments through three points: 0% to 80, the expected
     threshold to 100, and 100% to 120. It's between the other two options and
     needs no seed, but it loses the shape of the real curve.

  Which option would you like?

● Noted: the score comes from a seed of the GOV.UK 2025 conversion tables,
  interpolated between points.

  Fifth question: how should "as at a point in time" be stored?

  1. A history table with validity dates (my recommendation).
     - Responses are rolled up incrementally to one row per pupil, subject and
       sitting.
     - The score history is one row per pupil and subject each time a sitting
       changes their score, with valid_from and valid_to dates.
     - An "as at" lookup is where @ts >= valid_from and @ts < valid_to.
     - A current table, where valid_to is null, gives a clean shape to load into
       the application database.
     - Rows only grow with the number of sittings, not with responses or days,
       so it scales to more than 100m responses.
  2. A daily snapshot table, with one row per pupil, subject and day. It's very
     easy to query, but rows grow every day whether anything changed or not, and
     it only gives day-level accuracy.
  3. A parameterised table function, sats_scores_as_of(ts), that computes scores
     from responses on demand. It stores nothing, but it rescans the response
     data on every call. That works today but would be expensive at production
     volumes.

  By default I'll also add n_questions and a low_evidence flag (fewer than 20
  questions answered) so the app can show confidence, instead of hiding those
  pupils.

  Which option would you like?

  Next question: very low scores

     clamped.
